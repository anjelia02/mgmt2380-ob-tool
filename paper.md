# Unspoken Expectations: Making Psychological Contracts Visible at Work

## The Concept

Every job carries a set of expectations that nobody wrote down. A new analyst assumes her manager wants a reply within the working day. A manager assumes the team will raise problems early, and never says so. These beliefs about what each side owes the other are what organizational scholars call the psychological contract. Denise Rousseau defined it in 1989 as an individual's beliefs about the reciprocal obligations between that person and an organization (Rousseau, 1989). Over time the idea has come to describe the relationship between a worker and a direct manager, and that is where most of its daily weight sits.

The contract is psychological for two reasons. It lives in each person's head, not in a signed document. And the two parties can hold different versions of it without knowing. Rousseau (1995) separated transactional contracts, which trade specific exchanges such as pay for hours, from relational contracts, which are open-ended and built on loyalty, development, and long-term security. Most working relationships mix both. The mix matters. Relational expectations are the hardest to state. People rarely announce that they expect to hear when their work is slipping, or that they expect a manager to shield them from a bad surprise. They assume these things, and then feel let down when they do not happen.

## Research Behind the Concept

The research began with a simple observation: perceived breaches are common. Robinson and Rousseau (1994) surveyed graduate management alumni twice, at graduation and again two years later. Their title states the finding. Violation of the psychological contract was not the exception but the norm. Many respondents reported that their employers had failed to meet obligations they believed were owed to them, and those perceptions were tied to lower trust in the organization.

Morrison and Robinson (1997) then explained how a breach becomes a violation. A breach is a judgment: the person believes the other party has not delivered what was promised. A violation is the emotional reaction that follows, often anger, betrayal, or withdrawal. Their model shows that the gap usually opens before anyone speaks. One party forms an expectation, the other never learns of it, and the judgment forms in private. In their account, many breaches begin in missing communication rather than in bad intent.

A meta-analysis by Zhao, Wayne, Glibkowski, and Bravo (2007) gathered outcomes from many samples. Breach related to a range of attitudes and behaviors, including commitment and satisfaction, though the analysis found no link to actual turnover. The authors tested whether the type of contract changed these results. For a practitioner, the lesson is plain. Unspoken expectations shape how people feel about their work, and they shape what those people do next.

Research on misperceived norms supports the same conclusion from another direction. Prentice and Miller (1993) found that students believed they were more uncomfortable with campus drinking norms than the typical student was. Each person assumed the exception was himself or herself, and that belief kept the norm in place. The pattern, which the authors describe as pluralistic ignorance, shows how a group can sustain an expectation that most of its members privately reject.

Research on voice adds a third strand. Detert and Edmondson (2011) argued that employees often hold implicit beliefs, such as the belief that speaking up is pointless or risky, and these beliefs produce silence even when people have useful information. Morrison and Milliken (2000) described organizational silence as a shared pattern, held up by beliefs about what happens when people speak. Edmondson (1999) linked psychological safety, a shared sense that interpersonal risk is tolerable, to learning behavior in teams. Taken together, these findings suggest that expectations about speaking up are themselves unspoken expectations.

## Why the Concept Matters at Work

Unspoken expectations matter most at points of change. Onboarding, a new manager, a shift in role, and a performance review each reopen the question of what each side owes the other. Old answers may no longer apply. The cost of silence grows with time. A small gap in the first month can become a grievance by the sixth.

The concept applies widely. Most employees have a manager, and most managers lead a team. The same structure appears between a junior and a senior colleague, a contractor and a client, or a student and an advisor. That breadth is the reason to build a practical tool around it.

## The Tool

The tool, titled Unspoken Expectations, is a short interactive web page with five steps. First, each person answers four questions about eight everyday workplace items. The first asks whether the item was promised, agreed to, or clearly implied. The second and third ask how strongly the person believes their partner owes them the item, and how strongly they believe they owe it to their partner. The fourth asks how strongly they think their partner believes the partner owes them the item. The questions separate a want from a belief about an obligation, since a person can want a behavior without believing anyone promised it. Second, each person receives a code that holds only the answers and no names. Third, the reveal shows the answers after both people have shared their codes. It marks items where the two people see the promise differently, items where their views of what is owed do not match, and items where one person's guess about the other was far off. Fourth, the tool proposes an opening question for each of the three largest differences. Fifth, the pair writes one agreement per difference and sets a date to check back in.

Each step connects to the research above. The guessing step follows the logic of pluralistic ignorance. People often act on what they assume others think, so the tool asks for those assumptions directly and then tests them against the other person's answers. The reveal treats gaps as questions rather than verdicts. This follows Morrison and Robinson's account of breaches that form in silence. The question-based openers draw on the voice research. A question invites an answer rather than a verdict, so it may lower the risk the other person feels when responding. This reasoning extends the voice findings into a new setting, and the tool does not test it. The check-in date reflects the view that a psychological contract changes over time, so any agreement needs review.

## Limits and Testing

The prototype has not yet been evaluated with users, and its design brings limits that a reader should weigh. The reveal depends on both people taking part. A person who completes only the first step still gets the guessing task, but the reveal stays locked. The codes travel by copy and paste, so people must share them through a channel they trust. The page enforces the reveal in the interface rather than on a secure server. That choice suits a classroom project but would not suit a workplace with sensitive data.

A fair test would take a practical form. Three to five employees could complete the tool and answer two questions: whether it brought up a promise or obligation they had been avoiding, and whether the openers helped them start the conversation. Those answers would show whether the tool changes behavior. The current version cannot show that.

## Conclusion

Unspoken expectations shape working relationships whether or not anyone names them. Research on psychological contracts shows that breaches are common, that many begin with assumptions nobody checks, and that they affect how people feel and act. The tool offers a short path from assumption to conversation. It asks each person to guess, then checks the guess. It treats differences as material for discussion and records what the pair agrees to. Its real value depends on use in working relationships, and that test is the logical next step.
